Each case describes how the slide is built and how to reuse the pattern. Times refer to Jeff Su's video "5 presentation techniques from McKinsey, Bain, and BCG".

## 1. Past data makes the change visible (video 00:49)

**Build.** A two-column slide. The left column states the main finding in larger text. The right column lists five emotions, each with two horizontal bars side by side, 2023 in mint and 2018 in purple, with the percentage written inside each bar.

| Emotion | 2023 | 2018 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Curious | 61% | 60% |
| Optimistic | 52% | 35% |
| Worried | 30% | 40% |
| Confident | 26% | 17% |
| Indifferent | 14% | 21% |

**Why it works.** Optimism up 17 points and worry down 10 points is the story, and the paired bars let the eye see both changes in one glance. The reading order is finding, then data, then the change itself.

**Reuse.** Pair each current value with a past value, give each period one fixed color, and lead with the most important change. Then use that change to open the next slide with a question ("why did this change?"), answered by consumer AI tools making people familiar with the technology.

## 2. Result beside a benchmark (video 01:41)

**Build.** Bain's food-systems report highlights Ethiopia's 63% growth in per-capita grain production, with the 12% median of comparison countries immediately beside it.

**Why it works.** Result and benchmark share one field of view, so the reader sees the growth and its relative standing together.

**Reuse.** Combine the main metric and its reference into one compact block: a large number for the result, a short phrase for the benchmark. It suits company-versus-industry, country-versus-peers, and actual-versus-plan comparisons.

## 3. Executive summary paragraphs as SCQA (video 02:52)

**Build.** BCG's AI-at-work summary is a sequence of paragraphs. The first sets the situation (people are more positive about AI). The second states the complication (attitudes differ across groups and deep concerns persist). The third poses the question (what should be done to close the gap?). The rest of the report answers it and the last page gives three recommendations.

**Reuse.** Write the summary so each paragraph plays one SCQA role, then tie every later section back to the question. In a live talk, highlight one paragraph at a time. McKinsey's State of AI summary follows the same shape: generative AI is early but widespread (situation), many companies have not handled the risks while leaders move ahead (complication), what are the leaders doing about risk? (question).

## 4. Answer first, then reasons (video 05:14)

**Build.** Bain's metaverse report asks how long until the metaverse goes mainstream. The page opens with the answer, "five to ten more years", then shows how long smartphones, game consoles, the internet, and laptops took to reach mass adoption.

**Why it works.** The reader holds the conclusion before the evidence, then follows the reasoning downward.

**Reuse.** Put the answer in the title or the first line, follow with the key findings, and add the detail after. Order the whole deck the same way: what to do first, then what was found.

## 5. Position shows progression, color shows grouping (video 05:54)

**Build.** BCG's five-stage capability chart steps from lower left to upper right. Each step has a number and a stage name, with a description of the capability to its right, progressing from simple self-service transactions to a continuous assistant across the whole journey. Stages one and two are grey (existing capabilities), stages three to five are green (generative AI), and a bracket spans the green stages.

**Reuse.** Arrange stages in order of progression, give every stage in the same group one color, bracket the group, and write what the grouping means beside it. Position, number, color, and text each carry one distinct piece of the message.

## 6. One color per series across rows (video 06:24)

**Build.** McKinsey's industry chart lists industries as rows, each with a stacked bar showing usage status in six colors. Every row uses the same series order and the same color for each status. The video converts the slide to black and white first, then restores color, to show how color lets the eye find the parts to compare.

**Reuse.** Decide each series' meaning and color first, then apply it to every row. Order the legend to match the order in the bars. The reader learns the encoding once and follows one color down the page.

## 7. Color guides a text-heavy decision tree (video 06:48)

**Build.** BCG's model-selection tree flows from the left. Questions about capability needs, budget, and in-house talent branch to different options. Bold green keywords mark what each question concerns, a green "yes" and red "no" mark the branches, and yellow text marks notes.

**Reuse.** Turn a choice into a series of answerable questions, connect each answer to the next question or an option, and let color serve keywords, branch labels, and notes, so one path can be read from start to result even on a dense page.

## 8. A table for lookup (video 08:19)

**Build.** BCG's Most Innovative Companies 2023 page lists 50 companies with rank, name, change from last year, and status such as returning or new entrant. Companies are grouped by rank range (1-10, 11-20, and so on). The reader finds the range, then the company, then reads rank, change, and status along the row (Tesla up three places, Meta down five).

**Reuse.** Pick the fields the reader needs together, build a fixed object-to-field layout, and group long lists by range so name, rank, change, and status keep stable positions. Highlight a key row with a tint or bold name.
